Team Rattlers vs. Team Boomslang: Snake Class at Pocahontas Middle School

Oct 22, 2026 Jayakanthan, SaveSnakes.org 10 min read

The P.A.C.K. animal advocacy club's first meeting of the year, and thirty-some sixth graders who can now pick a copperhead out of a lineup faster than their parents can.

I walked into the library at Pocahontas Middle School on Monday, October 19, put a text message up on the screen, and did not introduce myself.

"HELP. Huge snake right next to the kids pool. My husband is getting the shovel. Is this a copperhead???"

Ten seconds. Stand up if you think it is a copperhead.

About half the room stood. I told them to sit back down, that we would come back to this one, and that by the end of the afternoon every one of them would get it right in under three seconds. Then I told them who I was: I get called to people's houses to remove snakes, I do it without hurting the snake, and for the next hour they were the rescue team.

This was the first meeting of the year for the P.A.C.K., the school's humane education and animal advocacy club. Debra Milby, the sixth grade counselor who sponsors it, had invited me to talk about native Virginia snakes, and more than 30 students had brought signed notes from home and arranged rides so they could stay after school for it. That is a lot of effort for a middle schooler to put into snakes. It told me before I said a word that this was going to be a good room.

The rules of the game

Every case in the session comes from a real call or text to my phone. Before I reveal anything, the room votes: standing, hands, or shouting. No vote, no learning.

Then we split the room down the middle and each side picked a team name. It had to be a snake. Thirty seconds. That is how I ended up refereeing Team Rattlers against Team Boomslang. (A boomslang is an African tree snake. Nobody in Virginia will ever meet one. The kids did not care, and the name was too good to overrule.)

I kept score on the whiteboard and put my phone away in front of the room: for the next hour I was not answering calls, so if a snake turned up in Henrico, they were on their own.

One number that changes everything

First round, guess the numbers. How many kinds of snakes live in Virginia? Most guesses came in low. Thirty-two. How many are venomous? Most guesses came in high. Three. How many of those three live in Henrico?

One.

I let that sit. You do not have to learn thirty snakes. You have to learn one.

Then the map round: one student from each team came up to point at where the timber rattlesnake, the cottonmouth and the copperhead actually live. The rattlesnake is in the mountains, with a rare population down in the Great Dismal Swamp. The cottonmouth is far southeastern Virginia, in the swamps. The copperhead is almost all of Virginia, including right here. Find the star for Richmond and only one map is colored. If it is venomous here, it is a copperhead.

Two questions, ten feet

The only lecture slide in the whole deck. Two questions settle it.

  1. Are the bands hourglasses? Wide at the belly, pinched across the back. Like a row of Hershey's Kisses lying on their side. The whole room drew it in the air with both hands.
  2. Is the head plain copper? No stripes, no bands, no eye mask. Just copper.

Both yes, copperhead. Either no, not a copperhead. Not sure, treat it as one and back away.

I also told them what not to use, because their parents taught them wrong. "Triangle head" is a bad rule: harmless snakes flatten their heads when they are scared, and that rule gets good snakes killed. Pupil shape and heat pits are real, but you can only see them up close, and up close is where people get bitten. If you are close enough to check its pupils, you are standing in the wrong place.

Then we took the calls

Six cases, each one a real message to my phone, each one a vote before the reveal.

The pool. Same text from the cold open, same photo, now that they were trained. Far fewer stood up this time. They ran the two questions out loud: the blotches were messy, not hourglasses, and there was a dark band through the eyes. A juvenile eastern ratsnake, completely harmless, the best mouse-catcher in Virginia, and the most commonly killed snake in Richmond. It grows up into the big black snake they have all seen in trees. That dad had a shovel in his hand.

The creek. "It puffed up and struck at my son's stick. Moccasin??" This one is a trap, and they caught it: the map had already ruled out cottonmouth before anyone looked at the photo. Northern watersnake, bands wide on top and narrow at the belly, the exact reverse of a copperhead. All that puffing and striking is a bluff, and the bluff is exactly why they get killed.

The flowerbed. "Aggressive and striking at my dog. It looks like poisonous." There were no bands at all, just stripes running the long way down the body, and no venomous snake in Virginia has long stripes. Eastern gartersnake, the official state snake of Virginia. It eats slugs and worms, and if your family has a garden it is working for you.

The leaf pile. The hard one, and the most important. "My kid was raking leaves and saw this in the pile. Is it anything? It's not moving." Half the room could not find the snake in the photo at first. That is not a failure. That is the copperhead's entire plan. Yes, this one was real. It was not moving because that is what they do: they freeze and trust their camouflage. They do not chase, they do not charge, they wait for you to leave. Almost every bite happens because someone stepped on one they did not see or reached into a spot they could not see. So: look where you put your hands and feet, and rake with a tool, not your hands.

The shovel. The only case with no snake photo, because the danger here was not the snake. A teenager texted me: "My dad found a snake by the shed and he's about to kill it with a shovel. I don't think it's a copperhead but he won't listen to me. What do I say to him?" Sixty seconds with a partner. Most answers were some version of "tell him it's not a copperhead." Then I pushed: he did not listen the first time, so why would he listen now? The real move is not to win the identification argument. It is to say, "Dad, even if it IS a copperhead, this is how people get bitten." Most snakebites happen to people who were trying to kill or move the snake. A snake you walk away from cannot reach you. The kid who actually sent me that text said exactly that line, and the dad put the shovel down. One student in the room went a step further than the script: don't kill it even if it is venomous. That is the whole session in one sentence, and it came from a sixth grader.

The bite. "My nephew just got bit reaching under the deck. His uncle wants to cut it and suck the venom out like in the movies. What do we do RIGHT NOW?" Tone shift, calm and plain. Call 911. Keep him still. Rings and watches off before the swelling. Keep the arm at or above heart level, never hanging down. And the whole right-hand column of things the movies taught you, every one of which makes it worse: no cutting, no sucking, no tourniquet, no ice, and no going back under the deck for the snake. Hospitals do not need the snake. They need the patient. Copperhead bites hurt and need a hospital, but deaths are extremely rare and antivenom works.

Lightning rounds and a tape measure

In between the cases we ran lightning rounds: a snake photo on screen, five seconds, stand if it is a copperhead, sit if not, next. Ratsnakes, kingsnakes, a scarlet kingsnake, a brownsnake, a hognose, a milksnake, watersnakes, and a copperhead half hidden in leaves. Scored by team, and the room got fast.

Then a break on purpose, because they had been sitting for twenty minutes. One volunteer from each team paced out what they thought ten feet was, and we measured. Most kids underestimate it. Standing at the real ten feet: this is where you identify a snake from, and everything you did today you could do from here. Walking to arm's length: this is where people get bitten. Getting closer feels like the careful thing. It is the opposite. Curiosity at ten feet is science. Curiosity at ten inches is a hospital.

What happens if every snake in Henrico vanished?

The club built this chain themselves, one link at a time. Snakes vanish overnight. Mice and rats explode, because they can have babies every few weeks. They move into garages, gardens and houses. More mice means more ticks, and mice carry the ticks that spread Lyme disease. Hawks, owls and herons go hungry, because snakes are their food. Take out one link and the food chain breaks in both directions.

The bonus fact got the reaction it always gets: the eastern kingsnake eats copperheads and is resistant to their venom. So the next time someone kills a big black snake with yellow speckles, they may have just killed the copperhead's worst enemy.

Snakes are not the problem in your yard. They are the cleanup crew.

Then a fast myth-or-fact round. Mothballs keep snakes away: myth, illegal to use that way and toxic to kids and pets. Snakes will chase you: myth, they flee, and if one comes at you it is heading for cover behind you. A triangle head means venomous: myth. A dead snake can still bite: fact, by reflex, so leave it alone. One ratsnake eats a hundred-plus mice a year: fact. And the question under the game, after the mothballs: who told you that? How would we actually find out if it is true? A relative swearing by it is not evidence. A test is evidence. That is the moment an animal club turns into a science club.

Last case: a photo of a yard and a question. "There's a snake in my yard every week. Why?" Find four things wrong. Spilled bird seed, which feeds mice, which feed snakes. A woodpile on the ground against the house. Tall grass and clutter to hunt in. A gap under the shed to live in. A snake in your yard is a report card on your yard. The bird feeder surprises everyone, including the teacher.

Final score

Team Boomslang took it by two points. It was close the whole way, and Team Rattlers has a rematch coming.

Everybody earned the three rescue badges I promised at the start. Spot: you can pick a copperhead out of a lineup, from ten feet, with two questions. Act: you know what to do when you find a snake and what to do if someone is bitten. Protect: you know why killing snakes makes a yard worse, not safer.

You do not have to like snakes. You just have to stop killing the wrong ones. Now you are the person in your house who can tell the difference.

What they took home

Every student left with two things. The first is a pocket card. One side is the two-question copperhead check, with photos. The other side is the five steps for finding a snake, what to do and what not to do for a snakebite, Poison Control, and the number to text a photo to for a free ID. It fits in a wallet or a backpack pocket and it does not need a signal.

The second is a fridge magnet with the SaveSnakes rescue number, so that if a snake ever turns up somewhere it cannot stay, the whole family knows who to call before anyone reaches for a shovel.

Their mission this week

I asked for three small things in return, and they agreed to all of them.

  • Yard check tonight. Find the snake magnets: spilled bird seed, firewood against the house, tall grass or junk piles, gaps under sheds.
  • Quiz a grown-up. Show an adult a snake photo and ask, copperhead or not? Then teach them the two-question test.
  • Say the line. Next time someone grabs a shovel: "Even if you're right, this is how people get bitten."

Thirty students, each reaching a household and a few neighbors, is a few hundred people in Henrico who now have a card on the fridge and a kid in the house who can explain it. That is more people than I will reach on rescue calls in a year, and it is the version of this work I would like to do a lot more of.

My thanks to Ms. Milby and the P.A.C.K. for the invitation, to the school for the library, and to the parents who signed the notes and drove the pickups.
